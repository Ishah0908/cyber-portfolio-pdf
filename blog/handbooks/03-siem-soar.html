Detection Engineering That Catches Things, Not Just Logs Them

A SIEM full of rules nobody trusts is worse than no SIEM, because it costs money and breeds alert fatigue. Here is how I build detections as a product with a lifecycle, tie them to attacker behavior, and let SOAR handle the boring 80 percent.

// 01

The alert-fatigue trap

Every SOC I have walked into with a morale problem had the same root cause: a SIEM firing hundreds of alerts a day, most of them false, and analysts who had quietly learned to ignore the channel. The detections were not built. They were accumulated, one vendor default and one panicked "add a rule for that" at a time.

The mindset shift that fixes this is treating detection as a product, not a pile of rules. A product has owners, requirements, a test suite, a release process, and metrics. It gets deprecated when it stops earning its place. A pile of rules just grows until the team drowns. Everything in this handbook flows from that one idea.

The only metric that matters at first True-positive rate per detection. If a rule fires 200 times a week and 198 are noise, it is not a detection, it is a denial-of-service attack on your analysts. Measure this first, then ruthlessly tune or kill the worst offenders. A smaller set of trusted detections beats a large set of ignored ones every time.
// 02

Telemetry: garbage in, garbage out

You cannot detect what you cannot see, and you cannot correlate what is not normalized. Before writing a single rule, two questions: do I have the log source that would show this behavior, and is it parsed into consistent fields? The second question is where most programs quietly fail. A username appears as user in one source, account_name in another, and src_user in a third, and now your correlation across them is impossible.

check coverage: which log sources are actually arriving
spl> | tstats count WHERE index=* BY sourcetype, _time span=1h
  | timechart span=1h count BY sourcetype
sourcetype                last_seen     events/hr
WinEventLog:Security      14:00         48,210
linux:auditd              14:00          9,884
aws:cloudtrail            06:00              0   ← STOPPED 8h ago
spl> # a silent log source is a blind spot; alert on absence too
Detect the silence The most dangerous log source is the one that stopped arriving without anyone noticing, because an attacker who disables logging looks exactly like a feed that broke. Build a detection that fires when an expected source goes quiet. Absence of data is itself a signal.

Normalize to a schema. Whether you use the Elastic Common Schema, OCSF, or a vendor data model, the point is that user.name means the same thing everywhere. Map every source into it at ingest. This is unglamorous plumbing and it is the single highest-leverage thing you can do for detection quality.

// 03

Detection as a product: the lifecycle

Each detection moves through stages, and the discipline is that nothing skips straight to production firing real alerts.

Store detections as code in version control. A detection is a query, some metadata (which ATT&CK technique, what data source, who owns it), and a test case. Reviewed in pull requests, deployed by pipeline. This gives you history, rollback, and the ability to answer "why does this rule exist" two years later.

What a detection actually looks like as code

People hear "detection as code" and picture something complicated. It is not. It is a small structured file that travels with everything needed to understand, review, and test the rule. The metadata is as important as the query, because the metadata is what lets you answer the questions that come up at the worst times: which technique does this cover, why does it exist, who owns it, and what was it tested against. A detection without that context is a liability that no one will dare touch.

detections/credential-access/lsass-access.yml
title: Suspicious LSASS process access
id: a1b2-credaccess-lsass
status: production
owner: detection-eng@corp
attack: [T1003.001]   # OS Credential Dumping: LSASS
data_source: sysmon EventID 10 (ProcessAccess)
logic: |
  TargetImage endswith 'lsass.exe'
  AND GrantedAccess in ('0x1010','0x1410')
  AND SourceImage not in (allowed_av_edr_paths)
test: atomic T1003.001-1  # must fire on this, last verified 2026-05
false_positives: backup agents, some EDR self-checks
tuning: maintain allowed_av_edr_paths lookup

Now this rule is reviewable like any other code change, its history shows who changed the logic and why, and the test field means CI can prove it still fires before every release. When an analyst gets the alert at 3am, the metadata travels with it: they know it is credential dumping, they know the known false positives, they are not staring at an opaque rule wondering if it can be trusted.

The graveyard is part of the lifecycle The stage everyone skips is retirement. Detections rot: a log source changes format, a tool gets decommissioned, an attacker technique falls out of use, the false-positive rate creeps up until analysts mute it. A detection nobody trusts and nobody tunes is worse than no detection, because it costs compute and credibility. Schedule a regular review that explicitly asks "should this still exist," and be willing to kill rules. A program that only ever adds detections is one that is slowly drowning itself.

ATT&CK-driven content

Do not write detections for random bad things. Write them against the MITRE ATT&CK techniques most relevant to your threat model, and track your coverage as a map. This turns "we have 400 rules" into "we cover 60 percent of the techniques our likely adversaries use, and here are the gaps."

ATT&CK techniqueTelemetry neededDetection idea
T1110 Brute ForceAuth logsN failed then 1 success, same account, short window
T1059 Command/ScriptProcess + cmdlineEncoded PowerShell, suspicious parent-child (word spawning cmd)
T1021 Remote ServicesNetwork + authLateral RDP/SMB from a host that never does it
T1567 Exfil to WebProxy / DNS / netflowLarge outbound to a rare destination, off-hours
T1078 Valid AccountsAuth + geoImpossible travel, new device + sensitive resource

Here is a concrete one for the suspicious parent-child case, which catches a huge swath of initial-access and execution activity.

T1059 // office app spawning a shell (Splunk-style)
spl> index=endpoint EventCode=1
  parent_process_name IN ("winword.exe","excel.exe","outlook.exe")
  process_name IN ("cmd.exe","powershell.exe","wscript.exe")
| stats count min(_time) AS first BY host, user, parent_process_name, process_name
| `security_content_ctime(first)`
host        user     parent          process        count
FIN-WS-22   jdoe     winword.exe     powershell.exe     1
# a Word doc just launched PowerShell on a finance workstation
// 05

Correlation patterns that actually work

Single-event rules are easy and noisy. The detections that earn analyst trust usually correlate across events, sources, or time. A few patterns I reach for repeatedly.

Threshold then transition: not "a failed login" but "many failed logins followed by a success." The success after the storm is the signal. First-time-seen: this user has never logged in from this country, this service account has never called this API. Rarity is one of the strongest signals you have. Sequence: technique A then technique B then technique C on the same host in order, which is far higher-confidence than any one alone.

first-time-seen: new country for an account (KQL)
kql> SigninLogs
| where TimeGenerated > ago(1d)
| join kind=leftanti (
    SigninLogs
    | where TimeGenerated between (ago(90d) .. ago(1d))
    | distinct UserPrincipalName, Location
  ) on UserPrincipalName, Location
| where ResultType == 0
| project TimeGenerated, UserPrincipalName, Location, IPAddress
TimeGenerated      UserPrincipalName   Location   IPAddress
2026-06-01 03:14   svc-billing@corp    RU         185.x.x.x
# a service account, new country, 3am: that is your alert
Enrich before you alert A raw IP means nothing to a tired analyst at 3am. Enrich at detection time: geo, ASN, threat-intel reputation, whether the asset is a crown jewel, who owns the account. An alert that arrives with context gets triaged in two minutes. An alert that is just an IP and a timestamp gets snoozed.
// 06

SOAR decision gates

SOAR earns its keep by handling the repetitive 80 percent so humans focus on the 20 percent that needs judgment. The mistake teams make is trying to fully automate response on day one and then getting burned when the automation does something dumb at scale. The safer model is automate enrichment fully, gate consequential action behind a human.

Good candidates for full automation: enrich an alert, open a ticket, pull a device's recent process list, block a known-bad hash. Things that need a human gate: disabling a user account, isolating a production server, blocking an IP range that might contain customers. The rule of thumb is reversibility and blast radius. If a mistake is cheap and easy to undo, automate it. If it could take down payroll, gate it.

// 07

Testing your detections

A detection you have never tested is a hope, not a control. You do not know it fires until you make the behavior happen and watch it light up. This is where atomic tests come in: small, safe, scripted executions of a single ATT&CK technique that you run in a lab and confirm your detection catches.

run a controlled test of a technique, confirm the alert fires
$ # in an ISOLATED lab host, simulate T1059 office-spawns-shell
$ Invoke-AtomicTest T1059.001 -TestNumbers 1
Executing test: powershell encoded command
Done.

$ # now confirm in the SIEM that the detection triggered
spl> index=notable search_name="T1059 Office Spawns Shell" earliest=-10m
1 result, detection fired as expected ✓
# if it did NOT fire, the rule is broken; fix before trusting it
Only in a lab Atomic and adversary-simulation tests execute real attacker techniques. Run them only in isolated, authorized lab environments, never against production or anything you do not own. Get written authorization for any testing that touches shared infrastructure.

Make this continuous. A detection that worked last quarter can silently break when a log source changes format or a field gets renamed upstream. Automated, scheduled validation tells you the moment a detection goes dark, instead of discovering it during an incident.

// 08

Measuring a SOC without lying to yourself

The metrics most SOCs report are the ones that are easy to count, not the ones that mean anything. Number of alerts processed, tickets closed, mean time to close. These feel like productivity but they mostly measure how much noise you generate and how fast you can dismiss it. A SOC closing ten thousand alerts a week is not ten times better than one closing a thousand; it might just have ten times the false positives. Vanity metrics make a drowning team look busy and a healthy team look idle.

The metrics that actually tell you something are harder to game. True-positive rate per detection tells you whether your rules are signal or noise. Detection coverage against the techniques in your threat model tells you what you would miss. And the one I trust most is dwell time measured in your own exercises: when a red team or purple team gets a foothold, how many steps and how much time before a detection fires and an analyst acts. That number, tracked over quarters, is the closest thing to ground truth about whether your program is getting better.

Vanity metric (avoid leaning on)Honest metric (track instead)
Alerts processed per weekTrue-positive rate per detection
Total number of detection rulesATT&CK coverage of your threat model
Mean time to closeMean time to detect, then to contain
Tickets closed by analystDetections that fired in the last real exercise
Log volume ingestedCoverage gaps where you have no telemetry

Purple teaming is the feedback loop

The reason purple teaming matters more than any dashboard is that it closes the loop honestly. The red side runs a technique, the blue side checks whether anything fired, and where nothing did, you have found a real gap and you build a detection for it on the spot. Do this regularly and your coverage map stops being a guess and becomes evidence. It also keeps the team's skills sharp and turns the relationship between offense and defense from adversarial scorekeeping into shared improvement, which is the entire point. A detection program that has never been tested against a live adversary, even a friendly one, is a set of assumptions in a trench coat.

The number to put in front of leadership When an executive asks "are we more secure than last year," do not answer with alert counts. Answer with the dwell-time trend from your exercises: last year it took a simulated attacker six steps and two days to reach crown jewels before we caught them, this year it takes nine steps and we catch them in hours. That is a sentence a board understands, and it is honest, because you measured it instead of counting tickets.
// 09

What good looks like

A healthy detection program is small, trusted, and measured. Every detection maps to a technique you care about, has an owner, ships through a pipeline, and has a test that proves it works. Analysts trust the alert channel because the noise has been tuned out. SOAR quietly handles enrichment and the reversible actions, leaving humans for the judgment calls. And you can draw your ATT&CK coverage on a map and point at the gaps you are working on next.

You get there not by buying a better SIEM but by treating detection like the engineering discipline it is. Build, test, measure, retire. Repeat.
