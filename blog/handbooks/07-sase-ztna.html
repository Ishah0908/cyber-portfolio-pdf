SASE & ZTNA Without the Hype

// 01

What problem SASE actually solves

SASE (Secure Access Service Edge) exists because the old model broke. That model backhauled every remote user's traffic through a corporate data center to apply security, then sent it back out to the internet. When the apps lived in the data center, that was fine. Now the apps live in the cloud and the users live at home, and hairpinning their Zoom traffic through headquarters to inspect it is slow, expensive, and pointless.

SASE flips it: move the security controls to the cloud edge, close to the user, and inspect traffic there instead of backhauling. It is less a product than a convergence of networking and security functions into one cloud-delivered layer. The value is real; the marketing is overheated. Your job is separating the two.

SASE is a journey, not a SKU No single purchase makes you "SASE." It is a target architecture you converge toward, often over years, frequently from multiple vendors. Anyone selling you SASE-in-a-box is selling the marketing. Buy the capabilities you need in the order your risk demands.

The acronym soup, translated

Every SASE deck throws the same letters at you. Here is what each one actually does and the question it answers.

ComponentWhat it doesThe question it answers
SD-WANSmart routing across linksHow does traffic get there fast and reliably?
SWGSecure web gateway: inspects web trafficIs this website / download safe?
CASBCloud access security brokerWhat are users doing in our SaaS apps?
FWaaSFirewall as a service, cloud-deliveredFirewall rules without a box, everywhere
DLPData loss prevention, inlineIs sensitive data leaving?
ZTNAZero Trust network accessShould this identity reach this app, right now?

The thing the deck will not tell you: you rarely need all of them on day one. Most organizations get the biggest risk reduction from ZTNA (replacing the VPN) and SWG (inspecting web and blocking the obvious bad), then layer in CASB and inline DLP as the data-protection program matures. Buy to your risk, not to the diagram.

// 03

ZTNA versus the VPN it replaces

ZTNA is the piece worth understanding deepest, because it is the direct application of Zero Trust to remote access and it fixes the VPN's original sin. A VPN, once you connect, drops you onto the network. You are inside. From there you can often see and reach far more than the one app you needed, which is exactly how a compromised remote user becomes a lateral-movement problem.

ZTNA inverts this. Instead of connecting you to a network, it brokers access to a specific application, after checking identity and device posture, and the app is never exposed to the network at all. You never get "inside." You get a tunnel to exactly the one resource policy allows, re-evaluated continuously.

// 04

Migrating off the VPN without a revolt

The VPN cutover is where SASE projects generate the most pain, because the VPN is load-bearing and everyone notices when remote access breaks. The migration that works is gradual and app-by-app, running ZTNA and VPN in parallel until the VPN has nothing left to do.

StageMoveWhy this order
1Stand up ZTNA beside the VPN, pick 2-3 pilot appsProve it with low-risk apps and friendly users
2Migrate the most-used internal web appsBiggest user-experience win, builds goodwill
3Migrate thick-client and legacy appsHardest cases, do them once you have momentum
4Shrink VPN to remaining edge cases, then retireVPN dies when its app list hits zero
Do not flip everyone at once The temptation is a hard cutover on a weekend. Resist it. A bad ZTNA policy that blocks a critical app for the whole company on a Monday morning will set the program back a year in political capital. Migrate per app, keep the VPN as the fallback until each app is proven, and communicate every step.

One operational detail that saves you: instrument the user experience from day one. The fastest way to lose support for a ZTNA rollout is users quietly deciding it is slower than the VPN, even when it is not. Measure connection time and app latency, and have the data ready when someone claims it got worse.

// 05

Operations and troubleshooting

Once SASE is live, the operational challenge shifts. When something is slow or broken, the failure could be in the user's local network, the SASE edge, the policy, or the app. A structured troubleshooting path saves hours of finger-pointing between teams and vendors.

isolate where the slowness lives, layer by layer
$ # 1. is it the user's path to the nearest SASE PoP?
$ mtr -rwc 50 <sase-pop-edge>
  loss%   avg   host
  0.0%    12ms  sase-pop-dfw   # clean path to the edge

$ # 2. is the policy adding latency, or is the app itself slow?
$ curl -w "dns:%{time_namelookup} conn:%{time_connect} ttfb:%{time_starttransfer}\n" \
  -o /dev/null -s https://internal-app.via-ztna
dns:0.03 conn:0.08 ttfb:2.41
# fast to connect, slow first byte = the app/origin is the problem,
# not the SASE layer. point the finger at the right team.
The troubleshooting order Always isolate in this order: local network, then path to the SASE edge, then policy evaluation, then origin app. Most "SASE is slow" tickets turn out to be a saturated home wifi or a slow origin app, not the edge. Having the data to show that quickly is what keeps the SASE team out of every unrelated performance war.
// 06

Single vendor or best of breed

This is the strategic fork every SASE program hits, and the vendors have strong opinions because their revenue depends on your answer. The single-vendor pitch is one console, one policy model, one support number, tightly integrated components that were built to work together. The best-of-breed pitch is the strongest tool in each category, no compromise on the part that matters most to you, no lock-in to one company's roadmap. Both are real arguments. Neither is universally correct.

The honest tradeoff: single-vendor SASE genuinely reduces operational complexity, and complexity is itself a security risk, so that simplicity has real defensive value. But you accept that some components will be merely adequate rather than excellent, and you concentrate your risk in one vendor's competence, pricing power, and the blast radius of their outages and breaches. Best-of-breed gets you the sharpest tool per category and avoids that concentration, but you pay in integration work, multiple policy models that can disagree, and the finger-pointing that happens when something breaks across a seam between two vendors.

DimensionSingle vendorBest of breed
Operational complexityLower, one consoleHigher, multiple seams
Capability per componentAdequate to goodBest available
Risk concentrationHigh, one basketSpread across vendors
Integration burdenVendor handles itYou own the seams
Negotiating leverageWeak, locked inStronger, swappable

My practical guidance: most organizations are better served leaning toward consolidation for the components where "good enough and well-integrated" beats "best but bolted on," while staying willing to break out the one or two capabilities where they have a genuine, specific requirement that the suite cannot meet. Let your actual risk drive the exceptions rather than buying best-of-breed everywhere out of principle or single-vendor everywhere out of convenience. And whatever you choose, keep enough portability that you are never truly hostage to one renewal conversation.

// 07

How SASE programs quietly go wrong

Beyond the architecture, there are recurring ways these programs disappoint, and they are rarely the things the design review worried about. Knowing them in advance is cheap insurance.

The latency surprise

Routing traffic through a cloud edge instead of straight out can add latency if a user is far from the nearest point of presence, and a handful of vocal users in an underserved region can sour the whole organization's perception. Check the provider's point-of-presence map against where your people actually are before you sign, not after the complaints start.

The encrypted-traffic blind spot

A SWG that does not decrypt and inspect TLS sees almost nothing, because nearly all traffic is encrypted now. But decryption is operationally heavy, breaks certificate-pinned apps, and raises real privacy questions you have to answer for your workforce and possibly your works council. Teams that skip the hard decryption conversation end up with an expensive inspection layer that inspects nothing.

The policy sprawl that recreates the old mess

The promise of SASE is unified policy. The reality, a couple of years in, is often hundreds of accreted rules that nobody fully understands, which is exactly the firewall-rule swamp you were trying to escape, just relocated to the cloud. Manage SASE policy as code with review and periodic pruning, or it rots the same way every rule base before it did.

You moved the single point of failure, you did not remove it The most important thing to internalize operationally: when your security and connectivity both run through a cloud edge, that edge becomes critical infrastructure for your entire workforce. If it has a bad day, everyone is affected at once, not just one site. Understand the provider's resilience model, know what your failure mode is when the edge is unreachable (fail-open and exposed, or fail-closed and unable to work), and make that a deliberate decision rather than a discovery you make during their next outage.
// 08

What to actually buy, and when

Cut through the convergence marketing with a simple sequence. Start with ZTNA to kill the VPN and shrink your remote-access blast radius, because that is the biggest single risk reduction. Add a SWG to inspect web traffic and block the obvious threats. Layer in CASB once you care about what happens inside your SaaS apps, and inline DLP as your data-protection program matures. SD-WAN and FWaaS slot in based on your networking refresh cycle, not on a vendor's roadmap.

You do not have to buy the whole diagram, and you should not buy it from one vendor just because the slide shows it as one box. Converge toward the architecture at the pace your risk and your budget actually justify. The VPN replacement alone will pay for itself in reduced lateral-movement risk; everything after that is incremental.
