// 01

What you are building

I built my first drone because I wanted to understand the whole stack, solder up, not just fly something off a shelf. The first frame I printed cracked on its second hard landing. The first time I trusted software in the flight loop, it twitched and I learned why you never do that. This handbook is the guide I wish I'd had on day one: every part, every wire, every command, and the mistakes that cost me a weekend so they don't cost you one.

Most "drone build" guides stop at a radio controller and line-of-sight flying. This one goes further: a quadcopter with two brains. A flight controller handles the fast, life-or-death loop, keeping the aircraft stable. A Raspberry Pi companion computer rides along for everything smart, and a 4G/LTE modem with a SIM card gives the whole thing an internet connection in the air. The result is a drone you can command, monitor, and stream video from using a laptop anywhere with a signal, and that can optionally make its own decisions through the Claude AI API.

The split matters. You never want a cloud round-trip or a Python script in the loop that keeps the props spinning, that is the flight controller's job and it must work even if everything else dies. The Pi and the cellular link sit on top, sending high-level commands ("go to this waypoint", "hold", "return home") and pulling back telemetry and video. If the Pi crashes, the aircraft still flies and fails safe.

The mental model Flight controller = reflexes (milliseconds, never offloaded). Raspberry Pi = the brain (seconds, does the thinking). Cellular link = the nervous system to your laptop. Claude API = optional judgment for autonomy. Keep those four roles separate in your head and every wiring and software decision below makes sense.

Capabilities when you are done

  • Fly and monitor from a laptop ground control station over LTE, far past radio range.
  • Live video + telemetry (battery, GPS, attitude, signal) streamed back over cellular.
  • GPS waypoint missions, position hold, and automatic return-to-home failsafe.
  • Optional autonomy: feed telemetry and camera frames to Claude and let it propose actions inside hard safety limits.

System architecture

Here is the whole data path. Read it left to right for commands, and follow the green return path for telemetry and video coming back. The cellular link is the only wireless hop that matters for control; everything on the airframe is wired.

Two rules fall straight out of this diagram. One: the flight controller must be able to fly with the Pi unplugged. You test that on the bench before you ever trust the cellular path. Two: the Pi talks to the flight controller over a wired serial link (MAVLink), not the other way around. The Pi is a guest issuing requests; the flight controller decides whether they are safe.

Two ways to do the flight brain

There are two clean architectures, and this handbook works with either:

  • Standalone flight controller + companion Pi (recommended). A dedicated board, an F7 or a Pixhawk-class autopilot, runs ArduPilot/PX4 and is wired to the Pi over serial. The two stay independent: if the Pi dies, the Pixhawk still flies. This is the most robust and the most common serious setup.
  • A dedicated flight HAT on the Pi. Boards like the Navio2 or a Pixhawk-style autopilot HAT bolt straight onto the Raspberry Pi and turn it into the autopilot itself, no separate flight-controller board. Fewer parts and wires, but the flight brain and the companion brain now share one computer, so a Pi fault is a flight fault. Great for tidy, experimental builds where you accept that trade-off.
Which to pick If this is your first build or you care about failsafe behavior, use a standalone Pixhawk/F7 with the Pi as companion, the separation is the whole safety story. Reach for a flight HAT when you want the smallest, most integrated airframe and are comfortable that the Pi is now flight-critical. Everything else in this handbook, cellular link, laptop control, the Claude brain, is identical either way.

Or: write your own flight controller (the deep-end path)

There is a third path worth naming, because it teaches you more than any other: roll your own flight controller on a microcontroller. A Raspberry Pi Pico / Pico 2 with an MPU6050 gyro, reading an IBUS/ELRS receiver and running your own PID rate-mode loop, will absolutely fly a quad, and you will understand stabilization in your bones afterward. It is a fantastic learning project (open-source bases like Tim Hanewich's "Scout" are a great read).

Learn on it, don't deploy on it Hand-rolled firmware is the best way to learn and the worst way to deploy. ArduPilot and PX4 represent thousands of hours of failsafe logic, EKF tuning, and crash testing you cannot reproduce in a summer. Build the Pico controller on a tethered test rig to understand PID, then fly the real missions on a proven autopilot. Best of both worlds.

// 04

Tools & workspace

You do not need a lab. You do need a few right tools, trying to build a drone without a real soldering iron is how people give up.

  • Temperature-controlled soldering iron (~$40) + thin solder + flux. The single most important tool.
  • Hex drivers (1.5/2.0/2.5mm) and a small Phillips. Frame screws are M2/M3.
  • Multimeter (~$20) to check continuity and catch shorts before you connect a battery.
  • Smoke stopper / current-limited supply for first power-on. A $10 part that saves a $200 mistake.
  • Zip ties, double-sided foam tape, heatshrink, helping hands.
  • A clear bench, good light, and a LiPo-safe charging area away from anything flammable.
Order of operations Charge nothing and connect no battery until the very end of assembly. Most magic-smoke moments happen because someone powered a half-soldered board to "just check". Build it fully, check continuity with the multimeter, then power through a smoke stopper first.
// 05

Assembly, step by step

Build in this order. Each step leaves the aircraft in a known-good state you can check before moving on. The sequence is deliberate, power and the flight controller go in before the Pi, so you can prove the aircraft flies on its own first.

1. Frame and motors

Assemble the frame. Mount the four motors on the arms and route their three wires toward the center. Note motor spin directions, the build instructions or your flight-controller software will tell you which two must spin reversed. Leave wires slightly long; you trim on final solder.

2. ESC, power distribution, and soldering

Solder the four motor triples to the 4-in-1 ESC. Solder the XT60 battery lead and the ESC's power pads, observing polarity, reversing this is the classic "magic smoke" mistake. Add a BEC if your Pi/peripherals need a clean 5V rail separate from the flight controller.

Polarity = no second chances Red to positive, black to negative, every time. A reversed battery lead will instantly kill your ESC and flight controller. Check polarity with the multimeter before the battery ever touches the connector, and do the first power-up through a smoke stopper.

3. Flight controller, GPS, and radio receiver

Mount the flight controller on soft standoffs (vibration isolation matters). Connect the ESC signal/telemetry, the GPS+compass to a UART, and the ELRS receiver to another UART. Keep the GPS away from power wires to avoid compass interference. This is the aircraft's nervous system, take your time on clean wiring.

4. Raspberry Pi, LTE HAT, and camera

Mount the Pi and its LTE HAT on the top plate with foam tape and a strap. Connect the Pi to the flight controller with a serial (TELEM) link: flight-controller TX→Pi RX, RX→TX, and a common ground, three wires. Power the Pi from the BEC, not a flimsy USB lead. Attach the camera (Pi CSI ribbon or USB) and screw on the LTE antenna.

flight-controller ↔ Pi serial wiring
# 3 wires, cross TX/RX, share ground
FC TELEM2 TX  →  Pi GPIO15 (RX)
FC TELEM2 RX  ←  Pi GPIO14 (TX)
FC GND        —  Pi GND
# baud 921600, MAVLink2 — set in the FC's serial config
What broke for me, so it doesn't for you 3D-printed frames are for prototyping, not flying. Mine cracked on a hard landing, fine for fit-checking the layout, but cut the real airframe from carbon fiber. Vibration eats GPS and gyros; soft-mount the flight controller and keep the GPS on a mast away from power wires. Cheap connectors melt under current, solder clean joints and use proper XT60s. And the protocol matters: a tidy digital link (IBUS/CRSF) to the receiver beats a fistful of PWM wires and updates far faster.

5. Calibrate and bench test (no props)

Before propellers, connect the radio and flight-controller software, then calibrate the accelerometer, compass, ESCs, and radio. Confirm the motors respond to throttle and spin the correct direction. Props stay off until every check passes and you have a working return-to-home and low-battery failsafe configured.

Flashing & software install

Two pieces of software: firmware on the flight controller, and the companion stack on the Pi. The companion stack, the MAVLink bridge, video streamer, LTE manager, and optional AI agent, lives in a GitHub repo with a one-line installer so you are not assembling it by hand.

Flight-controller firmware

Flash ArduPilot (Copter) or PX4 with the vendor's configurator (Mission Planner or QGroundControl). Both are free, open source, and battle-tested. Pick ArduPilot if you want the richest failsafe and scripting options; PX4 if you prefer its developer tooling. Configure the airframe, calibrate sensors, and set failsafes here, not later.

Raspberry Pi OS and the companion stack

on the Raspberry Pi (Pi OS Lite, 64-bit)
$ git clone https://github.com/ishah0908/cellular-drone.git
$ cd cellular-drone && ./install.sh
[1/5] installing MAVLink bridge (mavproxy, pymavlink) ...
[2/5] installing video pipeline (gstreamer) ...........
[3/5] installing LTE manager (ModemManager, nmcli) ....
[4/5] installing remote-link (tailscale) ..............
[5/5] installing optional AI agent (anthropic) ........
✓ companion stack installed — `sudo reboot` to start services

The repo is the deliverable you hand to anyone building this: it wires the serial link to the flight controller, brings up the cellular connection on boot, starts a video stream, and exposes everything to your laptop. The README documents every service and a config.yml for your serial port, baud, camera, and SIM APN.

Crucially, it does not reinvent the wheel, it stands on proven open-source drone projects. The installer clones and pins known-good versions of the established tools, so you are building on code that thousands of aircraft already fly:

install.sh clones the projects this build relies on
# proven autopilot + ground/companion tooling, pinned to known-good tags
$ git clone https://github.com/ArduPilot/ardupilot.git        # autopilot firmware
$ git clone https://github.com/ArduPilot/pymavlink.git        # MAVLink in Python
$ git clone https://github.com/ArduPilot/MAVProxy.git         # ground station / link
$ git clone https://github.com/mavlink/mavlink-router.git     # route telemetry over LTE
✓ dependencies cloned + pinned — your config.yml wires them together

Your fork's job is the glue: a small service that bridges the flight controller's serial port to these tools, manages the LTE connection, runs the video pipeline, and (optionally) calls the Claude agent. You write maybe a few hundred lines; the heavy lifting is borrowed from projects that have been flying for a decade. If you want to learn the fundamentals, the microcontroller path in section 02 (a Pico-style controller you write yourself) is the opposite trade, and both belong in a serious builder's toolkit.

Why a repo, not a tarball Hardware drifts, ports change, modems differ. A repo lets the install script detect your Pi model and modem, pin known-good library versions, and lets you git pull fixes after a firmware bump, without re-doing the whole setup. Fork it and make it yours.
// 07

The cellular link & laptop control

This is the part that makes it special: the drone gets its own internet connection, so your laptop can reach it from anywhere. Two steps, bring up LTE, then make the Pi reachable across the mobile network's NAT.

Bring up the SIM

activate the 4G/LTE modem on the Pi
$ sudo nmcli con add type gsm ifname '*' con-name lte apn iot.1nce.net
$ sudo nmcli con up lte
✓ LTE up  ip 10.64.x.x  ·  rssi -71 dBm  ·  band B2
$ ping -c1 1.1.1.1
64 bytes from 1.1.1.1: time=48.2 ms   # the drone is online

Make the drone reachable (NAT traversal)

Mobile networks hide you behind carrier NAT, so you cannot just connect to the drone's IP. A mesh VPN like Tailscale or ZeroTier gives both the Pi and your laptop a stable private address that works through any NAT, no port forwarding, encrypted end to end.

stable private address over any network
$ curl -fsSL https://tailscale.com/install.sh | sh
$ sudo tailscale up --hostname drone-01
✓ drone-01 reachable at 100.97.x.x from your laptop, anywhere

Fly it from your laptop

With the link up, point any MAVLink ground control station (Mission Planner, QGroundControl, or MAVProxy) at the drone's private address. You get the full map, telemetry, and mission planning you would have over a radio link, except range is now "wherever there is signal".

on your laptop — connect the ground station
$ mavproxy.py --master=udp:100.97.x.x:14550 --console --map
online system 1
GPS: 3D fix (12 sats)   Batt: 16.4V  98%   Link: 1.0 (48ms)
Flight battery OK · GPS OK · EKF healthy · ready
Latency is real Cellular adds 40–120 ms and can hiccup. That is fine for high-level commands and monitoring; it is not fine for manual stick-flying acro. Keep the ELRS radio as your hands-on control and use the cellular link for missions, telemetry, and the AI layer. Always configure a link-loss failsafe (RTH or land) so a dropped signal never means a flyaway.

Want low-latency eyes? Add analog FPV

The cellular video is great for situational awareness but too laggy to fly by. If you want to fly by camera, add a classic analog FPV path alongside the cellular one: a small camera into an analog video transmitter (VTX) feeding FPV goggles. It is a separate, near-instant link, builders routinely hit ~50 ms glass-to-goggles, completely independent of the mobile network. Use cellular for the brain and the map; use analog FPV for your eyes.

Adding a Claude AI brain

Now the interesting part. Because the Pi has internet, it can ask Claude for judgment: interpret a scene, choose between waypoints, summarize what the camera sees, or decide whether conditions are safe to continue. Claude proposes; your code enforces hard limits and the flight controller has final say. The AI is an advisor with a seatbelt, never a direct hand on the motors.

ai_agent.py — telemetry in, a bounded decision out
$ export ANTHROPIC_API_KEY=sk-ant-...

import os, json, anthropic
client = anthropic.Anthropic(api_key=os.environ["ANTHROPIC_API_KEY"])

RULES = """You are a flight advisor. Reply ONLY as JSON:
{"action": "continue|hold|return", "reason": "..."}.
Rules: if battery < 30% -> return. If wind high or GPS < 3D -> hold."""

def decide(telemetry):
    msg = client.messages.create(
        model="claude-opus-4-8",
        max_tokens=120,
        system=[{"type":"text","text":RULES,
                 "cache_control":{"type":"ephemeral"}}],  # cache the rules
        messages=[{"role":"user","content":json.dumps(telemetry)}],
    )
    return json.loads(msg.content[0].text)

>>> decide({"batt_pct":27,"gps":"3D","wind":"calm"})
{"action": "return", "reason": "battery below 30% threshold"}

That snippet is the whole idea: structured telemetry goes up, a small JSON decision comes back, and your code on the Pi only ever acts on it after re-checking the same limits locally. Caching the rules block keeps repeated calls fast and cheap. Swap claude-opus-4-8 for a smaller model when you want lower latency on routine checks.

Hard limits live in code, not in the prompt Never let the model command throttle or attitude directly, and never trust it as your only safety check. Battery, geofence, altitude ceiling, and return-to-home must be enforced by the flight controller and by plain if statements on the Pi. Treat Claude's output as a suggestion you validate, exactly as you would a junior pilot's call.

Good first uses: narrate what the camera sees into the telemetry log, flag "this looks unsafe, recommend hold", pick the next survey waypoint from a list, or summarize a flight afterward. Save direct autonomous maneuvering for after many hours of supervised testing in open, legal airspace.

// 09

Pre-flight, testing & first flight

The build is done; resist the urge to fling it into the sky. Earn the first flight with checks.

Bench, props off

  • Motors spin the right direction and respond to throttle; reverse any that are wrong in software.
  • Radio failsafe triggers RTH/land when you power off the transmitter.
  • Cellular link comes up on boot and the laptop GCS shows live telemetry.
  • Low-battery and link-loss failsafes are configured and tested.

First hover

  • Props on, open area, away from people, legal airspace, full battery.
  • Arm, lift to ~1 meter on the radio, hover a few seconds, land. Feel for drift or wobble.
  • Only after stable manual hovering: test GPS position hold, then a single short waypoint, then the cellular/AI layer.
Build the habit, not just the drone Every flight: visual airframe check, prop tightness, battery voltage, GPS lock, failsafe armed. The pilots who never have a bad day are the ones who never skip the boring checklist. A drone is a flying machine with spinning blades, respect it and it stays fun.

Total cost & where to go next

End to end, expect ~$700–$900 in hardware to go from an empty bench to a cellular, Pi-powered drone you fly from a laptop, plus about $10/month for the SIM data and pennies per call if you use the Claude API. You can start cheaper and grow into it.

Where to take it once it flies: autonomous survey grids, a return-to-home triggered by Claude reading the scene, on-board recording with a post-flight AI summary, or a second airframe so you always have one flying while one charges. The platform you just built, wired flight controller, Pi brain, cellular nervous system, AI judgment, is the same shape used by serious long-range and BVLOS systems. You now understand it from the solder up.
